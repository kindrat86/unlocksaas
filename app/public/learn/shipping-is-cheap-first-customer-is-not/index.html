Shipping Is Cheap. Getting Your First Customer Is Not.

Stripe-verified numbers from one of the best-known launch products in indie software show a sharp, sustained decline. The lesson is not that boilerplates are dead. It is that the expensive part of a software business has moved.

TL;DR: ShipFast, the Next.js boilerplate that defined fast SaaS launches, made $3,039 in August 2026, down 82.3% from $17,185 in January, according to Stripe-verified TrustMRR data. The product still sells and has more than $1.26M in lifetime revenue. But the setup work it packaged is no longer scarce. The scarce skill now is winning the first paying customer, and that job happens mostly after launch.

ShipFast is a Next.js SaaS boilerplate sold by Marc Lou, launched in 2023. Instead of wiring authentication, payments, and a database yourself, you bought a working stack. It became one of the most successful products in the indie category, with $1,269,704 in verified lifetime revenue.

August 2026 was $3,039: down 37.1% from July, down 82.3% from January, and down 85.0% from the December 2025 peak of $20,281. The trailing-30-day snapshot on September 1 sat at $3,288, a 33.7% decline from the prior 30 days. Marc Lou has separately reported roughly $3K for ShipFast in August, consistent with the verified figure.

What the decline does not prove

Three things are true at the same time, and honest analysis has to hold all of them.

  • ShipFast is not dead. It was still transacting daily at the September 1 snapshot, with $498 in the last 24 hours and about 8,300 lifetime customers. A declining product with near-zero marginal cost is still a business.
  • The founder is not failing. Marc Lou reported $82,221 in total August 2026 revenue across his portfolio. The gap was filled by newer products, including TrustMRR and DataFast. One layer of his business compressed; he moved to the next one.
  • Reach did not save it. His X audience grew to roughly 379,000 followers during the same period the boilerplate declined. Distribution helps, but it does not immunize a product whose core value is becoming free.

So no, the data does not prove that AI killed ShipFast. Single-cause stories are sloppy. Saturation after years of boilerplate launches, one-time-purchase fatigue, and the founder's own attention shifting all played a part. But the largest force is clear enough to act on.

Why shipping got cheap

ShipFast's original pitch was quantified: skip the 20-plus hours of repetitive setup work before your app does anything. In 2023, that week of auth, payments, and database wiring was a real, expensive wall. Paying $199 to skip it was a bargain.

In 2026, that wall is mostly gone. Lovable, Bolt, and Replit Agent generate the setup layer, authentication, payments, schemas, deployment, from natural-language prompts. Cursor and Claude Code compress the work that stays inside a codebase. When the before state costs an afternoon instead of a week, the ceiling on what saved setup time is worth drops toward zero. The market is paying for that reality: verified ShipFast revenue fell from the high four figures per month to roughly $3,000 while the tools that absorb setup work scaled to millions of users.

Notice the asymmetry. The part of building that got cheap was the part between idea and deploy. The part that did not get cheap is everything after deploy.

The part that stayed expensive

No generation tool turns a deployed app into revenue. A stranger paying you still runs through the same unglamorous sequence it always did: one named person with a real problem, one specific offer, one piece of proof, one message, and the patience to handle silence. Browse any community of launchers and the pattern is blunt: the ratio of shipped apps to apps with a single paying customer is brutal, and the bottleneck now sits after the deploy button.

That is the problem the cheap-shipping era actually created. When anyone can ship in a weekend, shipping stops being the filter. The filter is the first customer.

Frequently asked questions

Did AI kill ShipFast?

No, and that claim is too simple. ShipFast still sells every day and has more than 1.26 million dollars in verified lifetime revenue. What the data supports is narrower: the setup work ShipFast packaged became much less scarce, and the price the market pays for saved setup time dropped. That is commoditization of a layer, not the death of a product.

Is a boilerplate still worth buying in 2026?

For some founders, yes. An opinionated structure still saves decisions, and ShipFast markets its codebase as context for AI coding assistants. The verified numbers simply say a boilerplate is no longer a competitive edge on its own. It gets you to the start line faster, not to revenue.
