## IH Post #1 — Pattern (Tuesday)

**Title:** The praise-without-payment trap I heard in 10 founder calls

**Body (~500 words):**

Two months ago I ran ten founder conversations because my own Stripe line wasn&apos;t moving. I had shipped four AI products with Lovable and Claude Code. Two paying users across all four. I told myself it was the product. Then I told myself it was the funnel. Then I went deep into SEO for almost a year.

What broke me out of the loop wasn&apos;t a course, a thread, or a book. It was sitting with ten other founders and hearing the exact same sentence from each of them.

The sentence: &ldquo;The product is great. Everyone says so. Nobody is paying.&rdquo;

I started writing the pattern down. Every single conversation hit the same beats:

1. Shipped something real. Lovable, Claude, Cursor, Bubble. Doesn&apos;t matter what.
2. Has a small group of enthusiastic users.
3. Stripe is flat or near-flat.
4. Working on &ldquo;one more feature&rdquo; or &ldquo;the marketing site.&rdquo;
5. Has not asked any specific human for money in the last 30 days.

The brutal compression: every one of us had a beta. None had a buyer.

Here&apos;s what I think is happening, and I want to be wrong if I am.

**Praise is not payment.** A friend who tells you the product is great is reporting on the *vibe* of the product, not on the *value* of the product. The vibe report is genuine. It is also useless as a signal. The only signal that matters is a credit card.

**Building is the only respectable form of avoidance.** It is the only activity that lets you tell yourself you&apos;re &ldquo;working on the business&rdquo; while you specifically don&apos;t talk to a customer. SEO is a more sophisticated version of the same hiding place. So is &ldquo;the marketing site&rdquo; — the way most of us approach it, the marketing site is just another thing to build.

**The work that closes the gap is not technical.** It&apos;s social. It&apos;s scary. It&apos;s &ldquo;hi, I noticed you have a problem; here is what I built; would you pay $19/mo for it?&rdquo; in DMs to ten real humans. Most of us would rather refactor for the third time.

I&apos;m sharing this because I suspect the pattern is bigger than ten conversations and I&apos;m curious what other founders here have seen.

Two questions for the comments:

1. Have you ever shipped something with praise from users but no real conversion? What did you do next?
2. Have you ever caught yourself building because it was easier than asking for money? Curious how you broke out.

— Maryan

## IH Post #2 — Personal (later in week 2)

**Title:** I spent 11 months on SEO so I wouldn&apos;t have to look at Stripe

**Body (~450 words):**

This is a confession post. I don&apos;t have a happy ending yet. I&apos;m sharing because the pattern I just noticed in myself might be in some of you too.

Late summer 2025, I shipped my first AI product. By Q4 I had shipped three more. Stripe was flat. I told myself the obvious thing: I needed more traffic. So I started a serious SEO project. Long-tail keywords. Programmatic landing pages. Schema markup. Backlink outreach. The works.

11 months later:
- 47 indexed pages
- 1,200 monthly impressions
- 9 clicks
- 0 customers

Here is the part I have not seen written about as honestly as I want to write it now: **SEO was not a strategy. It was a respectable place to hide.**

Working on SEO let me tell myself I was &ldquo;working on the business&rdquo; without ever having to ask a specific human if they would pay me. Every morning I could open my SEO dashboard, see traffic go up by 1%, and feel productive. I did not have to open Stripe. I did not have to write a cold DM. I did not have to hear a no.

The pattern I see in retrospect: every time the work I was doing produced clear measurable metrics that were NOT &ldquo;customers paying me,&rdquo; I was probably hiding.

Things that hide well:
- SEO when you have no product-market fit
- Landing page A/B tests when you have no traffic
- &ldquo;Build in public&rdquo; threads with no ask at the end
- Refactoring the codebase for the third time
- &ldquo;Just one more feature&rdquo; before launch

Things that don&apos;t hide well:
- Sending a DM to a specific person and asking for $X
- Posting your offer and watching nobody buy
- Following up

The brutal test I now run on myself daily: if I removed everything I&apos;m doing right now EXCEPT directly asking humans for money for the product I&apos;ve already shipped, how much time would that free up?

If the answer is &ldquo;a lot,&rdquo; I&apos;m hiding.

Question for the comments: what&apos;s your respectable hiding place been? I want to know if SEO was a uniquely embarrassing one or if this is a pattern.

— Maryan

## IH Post #3 — Process (week 2)

**Title:** What Step 5 of any founder-playbook actually looks like (and why every tool quietly skips it)

**Body (~600 words):**

I&apos;ve been mapping out what an end-to-end &ldquo;from shipped to first paying customer&rdquo; workflow looks like for a non-engineer founder. Seven steps shake out:

1. Pin your dream customer (real human, named, not a category).
2. Build your offer (specific result, defensible math, real timeframe).
3. Define your voice (Attractive Character, three lines of bio).
4. Write your copy (headline, sales page, OTO).
5. Send outreach (20+ messages to specific humans, tracked).
6. Track replies and conversations.
7. Verify the first paying customer in Stripe.

Every founder I&apos;ve talked to can finish Steps 1–4 in a weekend with the right tools. None of them have completed Step 5 in the last 30 days.

That is the pattern I want to share. Step 5 is the wall.

Here is why I think Step 5 is hard to build into any tool:

**It&apos;s hard to DO.** The audience for Step 5 — the customer-avoidant founder — is a tough user. If you build a tool that forces them to actually send the message, they leave. If you build a tool that lets them skip and click &ldquo;mark as done,&rdquo; they buy the tool, never send the message, and stay stuck. The customer-success rate is dismal either way.

**It cannot be automated without becoming spam.** &ldquo;Auto-DM your Dream 100&rdquo; tools are illegal in spirit and against the terms of service of every channel that matters. The tool generating the message and the human sending it have to be coupled, not collapsed.

**The verification problem is unsexy engineering.** &ldquo;User logs a link to their public post. Tool fetches the link, confirms the post exists, confirms it&apos;s by the user&apos;s handle, marks the action complete.&rdquo; That&apos;s a Stripe-webhook-class piece of infrastructure, but for outreach. Nobody builds it because everyone is racing toward Step 4-and-stop.

The conclusion I&apos;ve arrived at, and I&apos;m putting it out here so people can argue with me:

A tool that doesn&apos;t enforce Step 5 is selling Steps 1–4 — and Steps 1–4 are not what fixes a flat Stripe line.

I&apos;m curious what the IH community thinks. Has anyone built or used a tool that actually closes Step 5 well? What did it look like? Did you stick with it, or did it feel like nagging?

— Maryan

## IH Post #4 — Polarity (week 2)

**Title:** Validate-your-idea advice handed to a founder who already shipped is gaslighting

**Body (~400 words):**

This is going to read sharper than I intended, but I&apos;m going to leave it sharp because I think the softer version of this post is what kept me stuck for 11 months.

I shipped a real AI product in Q3 2025. I shipped three more by end of year. Each one works. Each one has paying users I can point at — small numbers, but real. The Stripe line was flat enough that I went looking for advice.

The advice I got, over and over, from courses, threads, podcasts, and well-meaning friends:

— &ldquo;Make sure you&apos;re solving a real problem.&rdquo;
— &ldquo;Have you done customer interviews?&rdquo;
— &ldquo;You should validate first before building more.&rdquo;
— &ldquo;Find your ideal customer profile.&rdquo;

The product exists. People are using it. Two are paying. I am past the point where &ldquo;validate your idea&rdquo; is useful advice. Handing me that advice now is gaslighting — it implies I have not yet done what I have demonstrably done.

The advice I needed, that almost nobody gave me: **&ldquo;The work that comes after shipping is selling, not validating. Stop refining the product. Start asking for money.&rdquo;**

I think this matters because the entire build-in-public ecosystem is structured around helping founders who haven&apos;t shipped yet. Once you ship, the same ecosystem stops being useful. The advice doesn&apos;t update. You get handed &ldquo;validate your idea&rdquo; like you&apos;re six months earlier than you are.

If you&apos;re a post-launch pre-revenue founder reading this: the next advice you take should pass a single test. Does it ASSUME the product already exists, or does it ask you to re-prove it does?

If it asks you to re-prove the product exists, it&apos;s for someone who hasn&apos;t built yours yet. Skip it.

— Maryan
